10 · The object marker "o" (wo)

Pattern: [object] wo [verb]. The thing before wo is what the action happens to. Ask with Nani wo [verb]-masu ka? = "What do you [verb]?"

11 · Travel: going places

Lesson 4 · 0820

1 · kore / sore / are — this, that, that-over-there

These three replace a noun (they mean "this one / that one"). Which one depends on distance: kore = near me · sore = near you · are = far from both of us.
korethis (near me)
sorethat (near you)
arethat over there (far from both)
Kore wa hon desu. — This is a book.
Kore wa nan desu ka? — What is this?
Sore wa nan desu ka? — Sore wa kouhii desu. — What's that (by you)? It's coffee.
Are wa hoteru desu. — That over there is a hotel.
Are kudasai. — That one, please.  Kore kudasai. — I'll take this one.

2 · kono / sono / ano — this + noun

Same distance idea, but these can't stand alone — they must come before a noun: kono ___ / sono ___ / ano ___.
konothis + noun (near me)
sonothat + noun (near you)
anothat + noun (over there)
Kono hon wa omoshiroi desu. — This book is interesting.
Sono baggu wa kawaii desu. — That bag (by you) is cute.
Ano hito wa dare desu ka? — Who is that person over there?
The key difference: kore replaces the noun ("Kore wa kawaii desu" = "This is cute"), while kono must be followed by the noun ("Kono inu wa kawaii desu" = "This dog is cute").

3 · "ka" also means "or"

Between two nouns, ka = "or": A ka B.
Ie ka kafe de kouhii wo nomimasu. — I drink coffee at home or a cafe.

4 · "no chikaku" — near a place

[place] no chikaku = "near [place]." no connects the two nouns.
Ie wa doko desu ka? — Eki no chikaku desu. — Where's your house? Near the station.
Ie no chikaku no suupaa ni ikimasu. — I go to the grocery store near my house.
Benri desu. — It's convenient.  Fuben desu. — It's inconvenient.

5 · Telling time: ji, han, sanjuppun

[number]-ji = o'clock; han = half past; [number]-ji [minutes] for exact times. Ask "what time?" with nanji.
Nanji ni okimasu ka? — Roku-ji han ni okimasu. — What time do you wake up? At 6:30.
Nanji ni nemasu ka? — Juu-ji ni nemasu. — What time do you sleep? At 10.
Soshite, asa roku-ji han ni okimasu. — And then I wake up at 6:30 in the morning.

2 · Which one? — dore / docchi + comparing

dore = which (of 3+), docchi = which (of two). Compare with ~ no hou ga = "the ~ side is more…". kocchi / socchi / acchi = this / that / that-over-there one. migi = right, hidari = left.
Hare to ame, docchi no hou ga suki desu ka? — Sunny or rainy, which do you prefer?

Fuyu to natsu, docchi no hou ga samui desu ka? — Winter or summer, which is colder?
Fuyu no hou ga samui desu. — Winter is colder.
Migi to hidari, docchi desu ka? — Right or left, which is it?

3 · Weather & seasons

tenki = weather, hare = sunny, ame = rain. Seasons: haru spring, natsu summer, tsuyu rainy season, aki autumn, fuyu winter.
Kyou no tenki wa dou desu ka? — How's the weather today?
Hare desu. Ii desu. — It's sunny. It's nice.

5 · Verb groups (dictionary form)

Group 1 (u-verbs): change the -i before masu back to the -u row (nomimasu→nomu, kikimasu→kiku, kaimasu→kau). Group 2 (ru-verbs): drop masu, add ru (tabemasu→taberu, mimasu→miru). Group 3 (irregular): only suru (to do) and kuru (to come).

6 · Before / after — ~ no mae ni / ~ no ato ni

[verb dictionary] mae ni = "before ~-ing"; sono ato = after that; soshite = and then.
Gohan wo taberu mae ni te wo araimasu. — I wash my hands before eating.
Neru mae ni hamigaki to furosu wo shimasu. — I brush and floss before sleeping.
Ashita oshigoto ni ikimasu. Soshite ie ni kaerimasu. Sono ato souji wo shimasu. — Tomorrow I go to work. Then I go home. After that I clean.

Grammar

Pick the particle

Read the everyday sentence, look at the verb at the end, and tap the particle for each blank. These sentences are polite (–masu / desu) — the safe way to talk to anyone. Instant feedback + why + hear it.

Which particle does the verb want?

The fastest fix for "which particle?" is to learn each verb with the particle it demands. Pick the particle this verb wants for its key noun.

How it works (the notes)

The wall most beginners hit — "how do I know what word goes first?" — comes from your brain still treating Japanese as English with the words rearranged. It isn't. It runs on a different idea, and once it clicks, the "what order?" problem mostly dissolves.

The one big idea: English uses word position to show meaning. Japanese uses particles — little tags glued to each word.

In English, "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" differ only by which slot each word sits in — position is the grammar. That's why you keep asking what goes first. But in Japanese the middle of the sentence is flexible. What locks in the meaning is the tiny particle stuck to each noun.

Particles = the job tags

watopic — "as for ___, here's the situation"
gasubject — the one doing it
woobject — the thing being acted on
nito / at — destination, time, or receiver
deat / by — where the action happens, or the tool used
towith / and
etoward — a direction

Two rules cover most of it

Rule 1 — the verb comes last. Always. That's your anchor. The sentence sets up the scene, then the verb drops at the end and resolves everything.
Rule 2 — anything that describes something comes before it. Adjectives before nouns, adverbs before verbs, and even whole clauses before the noun they describe.

A full sentence, chunk by chunk

Tap each chunk to hear it. Read it as a scene being built: when → with whom → where → what → (verb).

kinouyesterday (when)
tomodachi towith a friend (with whom)
toshokan deat the library (where)
hon woa book (what)
yondaread (the verb — lands last)
kinou tomodachi to toshokan de hon wo yonda — "Yesterday I read a book at the library with a friend."

Here's the magic: you can shuffle the middle — toshokan de kinou tomodachi to hon wo yonda — and it means the exact same thing, because "toshokan de" carries "at the library" wherever it sits. English can't do that.

Rule 2 in action (describe-then-noun)

akai kuruma — red car (same order as English)
kinou katta hon — literally "yesterday-bought book" → the book I bought yesterday

English tacks the description on after the noun ("the book that I bought yesterday"). Japanese always puts it in front. Once you trust this, it's more consistent than English, not less.

The mental reframe

Japanese sets up the scene first — when, with whom, where, what — and then the verb lands and resolves it. You're not decoding a scrambled English sentence; you're watching a scene get assembled, then finding out what happened. Even native speakers don't know how a sentence ends until the verb arrives — that suspended feeling isn't you being slow, it's just what the language feels like.

How to study so it sinks in

Learn in sentences, not single words. Word→English flashcards train the exact translation reflex you're trying to drop. Prefer whole phrases (your Phrases and Talk tabs are perfect for this).

Listen and read below your level, in bulk. Easy material you can't translate fast enough to keep up with forces direct understanding. Volume at ~95% comprehension beats struggling through hard material at 30%.

Chunk at the particle, out loud: kinou / tomodachi to / toshokan de / hon wo / yonda — not word by word. Repeating audio a beat behind ("shadowing") builds the rhythm before your brain can translate.

When you speak, pick the verb first. Decide what's happening, then ask what that verb needs — who, to what, where — and fill the slots. Building out from the verb stops you composing an English sentence and swapping pieces.

1 · The four give / receive verbs

ageruto give (to someone else — direction away from me)
kureru(someone) gives to me / my side — direction toward me
morauI receive / I got (I'm the receiver)
moraerucan receive / be eligible to get (potential of morau)
The key question is direction / viewpoint:
• ageru — the gift goes away from me (I give, or A gives to B).
• kureru — the gift comes toward me or my side (family, close friends).
• morau — spotlight on the receiver ("I got ___").
• moraeru — "can get / are eligible to get"; the receiver can even be someone else.

2 · morau — "I received"

Tanjoubi ni purezento wo moraimashita. — I got a present for my birthday.
Kaisha kara omiyage wo moraimashita. — I got a souvenir from the company.
Ima mo kazoku kara tanjoubi purezento wo moraimasu ka? — Do you still get birthday presents from family?
Morawanai desu. — No, I don't. (morawanai = don't receive)

3 · moraeru — "can get / are eligible to get"

Kaiin ni naru to, 10% OFF no kuupon ga moraemasu. — If you become a member, you can get a 10%-off coupon.
Kono ibento ni sanka suru to, muryou chiketto ga moraemasu. — If you join this event, you can get a free ticket.
Ankeeto ni kotaeru to, purezento ga moraemasu. — If you answer the survey, you can get a gift.
~to here means "if / when": [condition] to, [result]. Notice moraeru doesn't need to say "who" gets it — it works for anyone / third person.

4 · kureru — "(someone) gives to me / my side"

Tomodachi ga purezento wo kuremashita. — A friend gave me a present.
Ojiichan ga watashi ni okane wo kuremashita. — Grandpa gave me money.
Sensei ga imouto ni hon wo kuremashita. — The teacher gave my sister a book. (imouto = my side, so kureru fits)

5 · ageru — "give (to others)"

Tanaka-san ga Yamada-san ni purezento wo agemashita. — Tanaka gave Yamada a present. (both are outsiders → away from me)
Watashi ga ojiichan ni okane wo agemashita. — I gave grandpa money.

6 · Causative-passive: saserareru = "be made to do"

saserareru = saseru (make someone do) + rareru (passive). It means "I was made / forced to do something." The person who was forced is the topic.
Watashi wa haha ni yasai wo tabesaseraremashita. — I was made to eat vegetables by my mom.
Gakusei no toki, sensei ni mainichi kanji wo kakaseraremashita. — As a student, I was made to write kanji every day.
Kaisha de zangyou saseraremashita. — I was made to work overtime.
Minna no mae de utawaseraremashita. — I was made to sing in front of everyone.

7 · How to build it (by verb group)

Group 1 (u-verbs)kaku → kakaseru → kakaserareru (write) · yomu → yomaseru → yomaserareru (read) · matsu → mataseru → mataserareru (wait)
Group 2 (ru-verbs)taberu → tabesaseru → tabesaserareru (eat) · miru → misaseru → misaserareru (watch)
Group 3 (irregular)suru → saseru → saserareru (do) · kuru → kosaseru → kosaserareru (come)
Spoken short form (Group 1 only): the long -aserareru often shortens to -asareru.
kakaserareru → kakasareru · nomaserareru → nomasareru · ikaserareru → ikasareru · mataserareru → matasareru.
e.g. Kanji wo kakasareru. — I get made to write kanji. · Juppun kurai matasaremashita. — I was kept waiting about 10 minutes.

8 · "Please let me ~" — sasete kudasai

Very useful and polite: [verb causative] + te kudasai = "please let me ___."
Sukoshi kangaesasete kudasai. — Please let me think a bit.
Setsumei sasete kudasai. — Please let me explain.
Kakunin sasete kudasai. — Please let me confirm.

9 · "I got to ~ / was allowed to ~" — sasete morau

This joins today's two big pieces: saseru + morau = "I received the chance to do it" → "I got to ___."
Ibento ni sanka sasete moraimashita. — I got to take part in the event.
Kaisha de atarashii shigoto wo tantou sasete moraimashita. — I got to be in charge of new work at the company.
Sukoshi yasumasete moraimashita. — I got to rest a little.

10 · Passive vs causative-passive

Passive (ukemi): Sensei ni homeraremashita. — I was praised by the teacher. (teacher did it to me)
Causative-passive: Sensei ni happyou saseraremashita. — I was made to present by the teacher. (teacher made me do it myself)

11 · Potential "-rareru" vs passive "-rareru"

Same ending, told apart by the particle:
Potential: Sashimi ga taberaremasu. — I can eat sashimi. (ga = the thing you're able to do)
Passive: Sakana ni taberaremashita. — I was eaten by a fish. (ni = the doer)

Because / when (wider verbs)

Ashita hayaku okiru node, kyou wa sentaku wo shite, hayaku nemasu.

Hon wo yomu toki wa, koohii wo nonde, ongaku wo kiite, sukoshi yasumimasu.

How they're stacked (sentence 1):
[when] Asa shichi ji ni → [action] okite, → [object + action] koohii wo nonde, → [object + action] nihongo wo benkyou shite, → [final: sets the tense] shigoto ni ikimasu.
Swap the last verb to ikimashita and the entire chain becomes past — nothing else changes.

Part D — Describing restaurants (adjective + ga suki)

na-adjective + na + noun; i-adjective goes straight before the noun. Reason: na-adj + dakara / i-adj + kara.
na-adjectivesshizuka na · nigiyaka na · kirei na · benri na
i-adjectiveshiroi · ookii · chiisai · semai · oishii
Shizuka na resutoran ga suki desu. Shizuka dakara suki desu. — I like quiet restaurants. I like them because they're quiet.
Hiroi resutoran ga suki desu. Hiroi kara suki desu. — I like spacious restaurants. I like them because they're spacious.
Opposites to know: shizuka ↔ urusai (noisy) · hiroi ↔ semai · ookii ↔ chiisai.
